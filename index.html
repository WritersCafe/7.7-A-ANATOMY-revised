Anatomy of 7.7B

This experience helps teachers see the hidden thinking students must coordinate when analyzing how character qualities influence events and the resolution of conflict.

7.7B — Character Qualities, Events, and Conflict Resolution

TEKS: The student is expected to analyze how characters' qualities influence events and resolution of the conflict.

Big Purpose: Students are learning that plot does not just “happen.” Character qualities affect how a character responds to situations, which then shapes events and helps determine how the conflict is resolved.

The Hidden Choreography

NOTICE CHARACTER QUALITY
↓
TRACK INTERNAL QUALITY → EXTERNAL ACTION
↓
CONNECT ACTION TO EVENTS
↓
EXPLAIN HOW EVENTS SHIFT THE CONFLICT
↓
ANALYZE HOW THE QUALITY SHAPES THE RESOLUTION

This is the invisible thinking students must coordinate.

Step 1 — Read Like a Student

At the beginning of the story, Jonah is nervous about speaking in front of his class. When his group project begins falling apart, he notices that no one wants to organize the presentation.

Even though he feels unsure, Jonah carefully listens to everyone’s ideas, creates a plan, and encourages each person to take a part.

By the end, the group presents successfully, and Jonah realizes that being thoughtful and patient helped the group solve the problem.

Cognitive Choreography

These are the thinking moves students had to coordinate while analyzing how character qualities influence events and conflict resolution.

Move 1: Name the Quality That Matters

What the student’s mind has to do: Identify the character quality that actually influences the plot.

Student inner voice: “Jonah is not just nervous. His patience and thoughtfulness are what shape what happens.”

If this move breaks down: Students name random traits that do not influence events.

Teacher look-for: “This trait matters because…”

Move 2: Connect Internal Quality to External Action

What the student’s mind has to do: Explain how an internal quality leads to an external choice or behavior.

Student inner voice: “Because Jonah is patient, he listens and makes a plan.”

If this move breaks down: Students describe a trait but do not connect it to what the character does.

Teacher look-for: “Because he is ___, he ___.”

Move 3: Track What Changes Because of the Action

What the student’s mind has to do: Explain how the character’s action changes events.

Student inner voice: “Jonah’s plan helped the group get organized.”

If this move breaks down: Students summarize events without explaining cause and effect.

Teacher look-for: “This caused…” or “As a result…”

Move 4: Follow the Conflict Shift

What the student’s mind has to do: Track how the character’s actions affect the central problem.

Student inner voice: “The group was falling apart, but Jonah’s choices helped fix that problem.”

If this move breaks down: Students identify the conflict but cannot explain how it changes.

Teacher ask: “How did this choice affect the problem?”

Move 5: Analyze the Resolution

What the student’s mind has to do: Explain how the character’s qualities helped shape the ending.

Student inner voice: “The conflict was resolved because Jonah’s patience helped the group succeed.”

If this move breaks down: Students say the conflict ended but cannot explain why.

Teacher ask: “What quality helped bring the conflict to resolution?”

Why Students Can Still Struggle with 7.7B

This is why students can retell the story perfectly and still miss questions about character influence.

They may know what happened, but they may not understand what caused it to happen.

The Anatomy of a TEKS helps teachers stop asking only:

“Did they understand the plot?”

and start asking:

“Can they explain how character qualities shaped the events and resolution?”
